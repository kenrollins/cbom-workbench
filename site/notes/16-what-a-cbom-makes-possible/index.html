    What does a CBOM make possible once it exists?¶

    The answer¶

    An inventory that is a list can be read. A CBOM whose entries carry their evidence, sit in a dependency chain and are rebuilt from a record can be used, in four ways a spreadsheet cannot be. It can be projected again into whatever field list is asked for, without a new scan. It can be compared with yesterday's to say what changed. It can be read against a policy to say what comes first and what blocks it. And it can be handed to a language model as the context for its work — which entry, on which listener, of which service, served from which file, with what evidence — so that a model can trace a weakness to the line that causes it and draft the change. The same entry is then what the result is checked against: a verifier probes the listener the entry names and decides, with no model in that decision, whether the change landed.

    The concept¶

    A model asked "make this database post-quantum" with nothing else will write something plausible about the software in general. Given the entry, it is asked something narrower and checkable: this listener at this address accepts these key exchanges, seen by this tool; the service's files are these; this line causes this one; this is the only file you may edit and these are the only lines that file may hold. The CBOM supplies the facts, the policy the intent, and the verifier the test. The model supplies the one thing none of them can: reading a configuration it has not seen before and writing the change.

    That division is the project's measured position on models (note 13): a role goes to a model when a model reached a floor on it, and never the role of deciding whether a change worked.

    The mechanism¶

    What the CBOM enables How Where it is shown
    Projection A field list is a data file; the record is rebuilt and projected in milliseconds the CBOM page, section 3
    Comparison An observation's reference is its fingerprint; event references are left out the CBOM page, section 4; watch mode
    Prioritization The policy pack's rules over the entries and the owner's supplied facts the queues (note 07)
    A model's context The entry, its chain to the service, the service's files the trace and the draft, on each entry and each proposal
    Proof The entry's listener, probed four ways, on a copy before approval and on the estate after the change section (note 08)

    The path for a service with no renderer. For software the harness can render by rule, a rule writes the change. For the rest, which is most of a real estate, the change-plan role drafts it:

    1. The context is the CBOM: the listener's entries from the latest scan, the investigator's trace where there is one, and the service's build files with line numbers.
    2. The model is told one file is editable — a policy file the service reads last — and which lines the runner will write in it. Three calls are made and all are recorded; the first usable reply is the proposal, with the model as its actor.
    3. The runner starts a copy of the service from the scope's image on an internal twin network with the proposed file, and the change's own check runs against the copy.
    4. Only a rehearsal that meets the intent opens the way to approval. A person approves; the runner applies, refusing any model's proposal that has no such rehearsal in the ledger and in its own log; the verifier probes the estate, the service is recreated, and the verifier probes again.

    Worked example¶

    The records database, PostgreSQL 18 behind its TLS upgrade, holds benefits records with a 75-year lifetime. Its CBOM entries on 2026-10-21 (scan run 01M5FG9BJBCD7B6WNSM209P8XR) show it offering the hybrid group X25519MLKEM768 first and still accepting X25519, 2048-bit finite-field Diffie-Hellman and RSA key transport; its RSA certificate's key is the file /etc/pg-tls/server.crt. The queue rule placed its key establishment in the queue due 2030-12-31, not blocked on its supplier (PostgreSQL on OpenSSL 3.5 lists ML-KEM), with a validated module available and not in use. The investigator had traced the DHE group to the server's default — no live line sets it — on Nemotron 3.5 Super VL.

    The policy for the listener was set to require. Gemma 4 31B on four L4s, given those seven entries, the trace and the four build files, drafted the same change in three replies of about 5 s each:

    --- agency/generated/records-db/tls.conf
+ssl_groups = 'X25519MLKEM768'
+ssl_min_protocol_version = 'TLSv1.3'

    The minimum protocol is what matters: TLS 1.3 carries no RSA key transport and no finite-field DHE. Rehearsed on a copy at 172.16.107.30, the change was enforced in 33 s: the hybrid-only client accepted, a client offering every classical group refused, the witness finding only X25519MLKEM768, and a TLS 1.2 handshake refused.

    The counterexample is the plan that sets only the groups. On a copy with that file, a client offering every classical group over TLS 1.3 was refused — which, read alone, says "enforced" — while a TLS 1.2 client completed a handshake with DHE-RSA, and another with RSA key transport (lesson 36). A model that wrote that plan, or a person, would have been told it worked by a weaker check.

    Two changes now wait for the owner's approval, in one change run (01M5FHSAWHMY78JV1EJX1YQA87 on the deployment): the records database's, drafted again under the scope regenerated that afternoon and rehearsed enforced in 37 s, and the jump host's SSH key exchange, which a rule renders into one line (KexAlgorithms mlkem768x25519-sha256) and which rehearsed enforced on a copy — an ML-KEM-only client agreed a key exchange, a client offering every other one was refused, ssh-audit found only the hybrid. A model drafts where spike S3 measured the role; OpenSSH had no change-plan items in the spike and needs no interpretation, so a rule writes it. Neither change has been applied to the estate.

    Where the CBOM sits in NIST's crypto-agility plan¶

    NIST's white paper on crypto agility (CSWP 39-upd1, December 2025, updated June 2026) draws the plan an organization needs in order to change its cryptography repeatedly, as its Fig. 3. Crypto agility, in the paper's words, "describes the capabilities needed to replace and adapt cryptographic algorithms in protocols, applications, software, hardware, firmware, and infrastructures while preserving security and ongoing operations" (§1). The figure has five regions inside a loop: governance (standards, mandates, supply chains, threats, policies, stakeholders); assets (code, libraries, applications, files, protocols, systems); management tools that discover, assess, configure and enforce; data-centric risk management — an information repository feeding a risk analysis and prioritization engine feeding dashboards and measures; and the two outcomes it informs, mitigation and migration, which feed back to the tools.

    A CBOM is that information repository. It is also what the other regions need from each other: the tools write to it, the engine reads it with the policy, the outcome is checked against it. The workbench's first page draws the figure, credited and with NIST's own labels, and fills each region with what the workbench does there, read from the ledgers at render time:

    NIST region The workbench, on the mini-agency Who acts
    Governance OMB M-26-15 as a policy pack checked against the memo's text; the crypto policy; the owner's facts per service person, rule
    Assets services, listeners and images from the scans; call sites in one pinned repository scanner
    Management tools testssl.sh, ssh-audit, cbomkit-theia and the workbench's readers; rules; the runner; the verifier scanner, rule, model (the trace, the call-site read), verifier
    Information repository the hash-chained, ML-DSA-65-signed ledger and the CycloneDX 1.6 CBOM rebuilt from it rule
    Risk analysis and prioritization engine the queue, priority and blocker rules; conflicts settled by rule rule, model (explaining a conflict; a name the table lacks)
    Monitoring and measures the receipts: coverage against the key, changes landed, time-to-change verifier
    Mitigation items whose software cannot take post-quantum cryptography, marked for replacement rule
    Migration a change proposed, rehearsed on a copy, approved, applied and verified twice model (the draft), person, verifier

    Each of the figure's labels is lit, half-lit or dim for the estate shown, with the reason, so the frame doubles as a gap analysis.

    The figure also carries one weakness through the loop, step by step, as the ledgers recorded it. A scan finds records-db accepting X25519, 2048-bit finite-field Diffie-Hellman and RSA key transport. Rules link the listener to the service, and the owner's facts (a 75-year data lifetime, a high-value asset) attach to it. The OMB M-26-15 rules then put it in the 2030 key-establishment queue as a prioritized system. A model traces one finding to its cause, and a second model drafts the edit from the CBOM's findings and the files the CBOM links to the service. The edit is rehearsed on a copy, the copy is tried against each kind of client, a person approves, the runner applies it, and probes prove it on the wire twice. The next scan's CBOM shows the classical groups gone, with the signature queue still open, which is the plan's next step for the service. At each step the page says what the CBOM contributes. The models act in two steps of thirteen and never decide whether the change worked.

    What follows¶

    • Build the record before the model. The model's work here is a few seconds; what makes it usable is that the question it is asked comes from an inventory with evidence and a chain, and that its answer is checked by something that is not a model.
    • Constrain what a model may write, not what it may think. One editable file, a fixed set of lines, a runner that refuses anything else. The model is free to read everything and to be wrong; it cannot write a line the service would load and the shape does not allow.
    • Rehearse before approval. An approver who sees a draft beside its rehearsal verdict is approving a measured result, not a model's suggestion.
    • Make the verifier stricter than the plan. The test that matters is the one the easiest wrong plan would pass.

    Scope¶

    Covered: the mini-agency; one model-drafted change, for one PostgreSQL listener, rehearsed on a copy; the projection and comparison shown on the CBOM page. Not yet measured: the change applied to the estate and verified there, which waits for approval; drafts for OpenSSH and the other services; how often drafts are usable across many services. The change-plan role's floor was measured on spike S3's thirty items with the same prompt and no paragraph naming an editable file; each draft here is checked by rehearsal, not by that measurement.
