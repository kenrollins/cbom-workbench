    What is a CBOM, and what is in one?¶

    The answer¶

    A Cryptography Bill of Materials is an inventory of the cryptographic assets in a system — the algorithms, certificates, keys and protocols — written as a CycloneDX bill of materials, the same format used for software bills of materials, with cryptographic assets as a component type of their own and dependencies between them. CycloneDX is ratified by Ecma International as ECMA-424, and CBOM support has been part of it since version 1.6. That much is standard. What an agency must put in one is not yet fixed: OMB M-26-15 requires a central CBOM fed from three kinds of evidence, and Executive Order 14412 has told CISA and NIST to publish the minimum elements of a CBOM by March 2027. Until then each agency decides what its CBOM contains, and every vendor fills the gap with its own fields. This project's position is that a CBOM is worth having only when every entry carries its evidence, when it is a projection of a record rather than a document someone edits, and when it is the thing a change is made from and verified against.

    The concept¶

    A bill of materials for cryptography answers the question a spreadsheet cannot: what cryptography is actually in use, where, and what depends on it. The OWASP guide puts the purpose plainly: discovering, managing and reporting on cryptographic assets is "the first step on the migration journey to quantum-safe systems and applications", and the trouble is that cryptography is "typically buried deep within components". So a CBOM reuses the machinery built for software supply chains — CycloneDX's components, evidence and dependencies — and adds the vocabulary cryptography needs.

    Three ideas carry it:

    • A cryptographic asset is a component. CycloneDX 1.6 adds cryptographic-asset beside application, library, container, file and the rest. An algorithm, a certificate, a key, a protocol is a thing in the inventory with a name, a reference and properties.
    • Properties say which variant. The guide's example is the one that matters for this project: the presence of SHA-1 versus HMAC-SHA-1 "makes a difference", so a CBOM captures the parameter set, the curve, the mode, the key size, and the quantum security level, not just a family name.
    • Dependencies say what uses what. The guide distinguishes a library that provides an algorithm from an application that uses it, and a protocol that uses an algorithm (its example: "TLS 1.3 uses ECDH/secp256r1"). That is the chain from a listener to the key exchange it negotiates to the certificate it serves to the key file behind it.

    The mechanism¶

    CycloneDX 1.6's schema defines a CBOM precisely. The asset types and the fields that matter most, from the schema this project validates against:

    assetType Carries Fields the schema defines
    algorithm A cryptographic function primitive (one of fifteen: kem, key-agree, signature, pke, hash, mac, block-cipher, stream-cipher, ae, kdf, drbg, xof, combiner, other, unknown), parameterSetIdentifier, curve, mode, padding, cryptoFunctions, classicalSecurityLevel, nistQuantumSecurityLevel, executionEnvironment, implementationPlatform, certificationLevel
    certificate An X.509 or other certificate subjectName, issuerName, notValidBefore, notValidAfter, signatureAlgorithmRef, subjectPublicKeyRef, certificateFormat, certificateExtension
    protocol TLS, SSH, IPsec, IKE, SSTP, WPA type, version, cipherSuites, ikev2TransformTypes, cryptoRefArray
    related-crypto-material Keys and other material type (private-key, public-key, secret-key, key, ciphertext, signature, digest, nonce, seed, salt, shared-secret, token, password, credential, …), with size, format, state, and an algorithmRef

    Every cryptographic asset may carry an oid, the object identifier that names it unambiguously (ecdsa-with-SHA256 is 1.2.840.10045.4.3.2, whatever a tool calls it). Relationships go in the document's dependencies list: each entry names a component by its bom-ref and lists what it dependsOn and what it provides. Evidence — where the asset was seen — goes under evidence.occurrences, with a location.

    What the schema does not define is as important. There is no field for who owns an asset, when it must be migrated, which policy puts it in which queue, whether a file found in an image is actually in use, or what observation the entry rests on. CycloneDX's answer is properties: name-value pairs under a namespace the producer chooses. That is where a vendor's schema, or this project's, lives.

    What the policy requires¶

    OMB M-26-15, Appendix A, section 6, names the three sources an agency's automated inventory draws on — software composition analysis, static and dynamic testing "to find cryptographic functions in code", and network scanners "to detect protocols and cipher suites" — and says they populate "a central Cryptographic Bill of Materials". It does not say what fields that CBOM holds. Executive Order 14412 directs CISA and NIST to publish the minimum elements of a CBOM within 270 days of 2026-06-22, which is 2027-03-19. Until that is published, "a CBOM" means "a CycloneDX document with cryptographic assets in it", and two agencies' CBOMs need not agree on anything else.

    Worked example¶

    The workbench's CBOM for the eight-service mini-agency (scan run 01M5DG85J78FF3FVG3GDH26SWZ, 2026-10-21) is a CycloneDX 1.6 document of 254 components: 47 cryptographic assets seen on the wire, 182 in images, 9 listeners, 8 services and 8 images. By the standard's four asset types, 139 are related cryptographic material (keys, keystores), 62 algorithms, 19 certificates and 9 protocols. Its dependencies list carries the chain from each service to its listeners and images, from a listener to what it negotiates and serves, and from a served certificate to the file holding its key.

    One entry: the certificate the records database serves after PostgreSQL's TLS upgrade, as the document carries it (two long values shortened):

    Read it field by field, as the workbench's CBOM page does:

    Field Defined by Says From
    type, cryptoProperties.assetType CycloneDX a cryptographic asset, a certificate the scanner's observation
    certificateProperties.* CycloneDX whom it names, who signed it, when it is valid testssl.sh's parse of the served certificate
    evidence.occurrences CycloneDX where it was seen: the listener the same observation
    bom-ref, pqch:fingerprint CycloneDX / project the identity of the observation; the same certificate in the next scan has the same reference a hash of layer, subject, type, role, name and key
    pqch:evidence project the ledger event holding the tool's output, its path and SHA-256 the scan
    pqch:role, pqch:family, pqch:quantum-vulnerable project a signature, RSA, quantum-vulnerable the rule quantum/vulnerable/1 reading OMB M-26-15's table
    pqch:verifiable-by project a change to it is confirmed by parsing the served certificate the rule verifier/class/1

    The standard says what the asset is and where; the extension says what the entry rests on and what follows from it. The chain is in the standard's own terms: the listener listener:172.16.106.30:5432 depends on this certificate, and the certificate depends on the file /etc/pg-tls/server.crt in the records-db image, joined by a rule that found the same public key in both. Above the listener, the records-db application component carries what no scan can discover — mission function "benefits records", a data lifetime of 75 years, high-value asset, owner "Records office" — each labelled supplied, with the person and the event that recorded it, and what the policy rules made of it: prioritized by all three of the memo's criteria, its key establishment in the queue due 2030-12-31.

    Three things can be done with this document that a spreadsheet cannot do:

    • Project it again. A flat inventory of 62 rows, one per asset in use for key establishment or signatures, with the system, owner, queue and evidence, is rebuilt from the same 535 ledger events in 19 ms; no scanner runs. Its field list is a data file. When the minimum elements are published, meeting them is another such file.
    • Compare it. Two CBOMs of the same estate are compared component by component, because an observation's reference is its fingerprint: two consecutive scans of the unchanged estate compare as 254 unchanged.
    • Hand it to a model. The investigator is given one entry's observation, the service its chain leads to, and that service's build files, and asked which line causes it; for the jump host's X25519 entry it named line 4 of sshd_config in two steps and 23 s on Nemotron 3.5 Super VL. The answer is recorded as the model's claim beside the entry, never in it.

    Two baselines are written from the same ledger in the same format, so the workbench's CBOM can be scored against an answer key beside CBOMkit alone and all the scanners alone.

    What follows¶

    • Ask for the format, not the brand. A CBOM that is not CycloneDX 1.6 or later is a vendor's private inventory. The format is the one thing already settled, and it is what the tooling — CBOMkit, the viewers, the scanners — reads and writes.
    • Ask what each entry rests on. The schema allows a CBOM with no evidence at all. An entry without a tool, an observation and a hash is an assertion.
    • Expect the minimum elements, and plan for more. When CISA and NIST publish them, a CBOM built on evidence will meet them by projection; one built as a document will need re-collecting.
    • Ask to see one entry, field by field. Which fields are the standard's, which are the producer's, and what each rests on. A CBOM whose producer cannot answer that for every field is not auditable.
    • Treat the CBOM as a projection. Here it is rebuilt from the ledger on demand and never edited; a change to the estate is an event, and the CBOM follows. That is what makes it the thing a change is made from and verified against (note 08) and what an auditor can check (note 12).

    Scope¶

    Covered: the CycloneDX 1.6 CBOM model as the schema defines it, what M-26-15 and EO 14412 require of a federal CBOM today, and how this project's CBOM uses the standard parts and the extension namespace, on the eight-service mini-agency; the anatomy, the second projection and the comparison as the workbench's CBOM page shows them.

    Not covered: the minimum elements CISA and NIST will publish, which do not exist yet; CBOMs for firmware and hardware; the CycloneDX 1.7 changes; how commercial platforms map their inventories to the format. The EO 14412 figures are carried from the project's facts table, which records that they were read through a summarizing tool and are to be checked against the source before publication.
