Resolution. Carrying out a step of the plan: the change proposed from the policy, rehearsed on a copy, checked against every kind of client, approved by a person and proved on the wire, twice. Nothing here is the plan; it is the proof that a step of it can be done.

Each step of the plan that a setting can move is carried out here. The order is always the same: propose, rehearse on a copy of the service, see who the change would shut out, approve — a person, signed into the ledger — and then the runner applies it and probes on the wire decide whether it landed, before and after the service is recreated. No model decides that. Approve only a step the plan says is ready: the plan shows what each waits for.

The change, step by step

Enabled is not enforced

Adding the hybrid group to a server changes nothing for a client that does not offer it. So the verifier runs a pair: a client offering only the hybrid group must be accepted, and a client offering every classical group — not just the two the server used to have — must be refused. Both hold: enforced. Only the first: enabled-not-enforced, a real state and not a pass. A second tool enumerates what the listener accepts and can only lower the verdict; the check is repeated after the service is recreated; no model takes part. The ledger behind it is hash-chained and checkpointed with ML-DSA-65 by a signer that keeps the key where nothing else can read it. What "verified" means · What an auditable record contains.

The central crypto policy sets key establishment for the portal and the records database. For the portal a rule renders the change; for the database, whose software has no renderer, a model drafts it from the CBOM's entries and the investigator's trace, and the draft is rehearsed on a copy before anyone sees an approve button. Either way the change is proposed with its diff and the check that will count as verified, approved by a person, applied by the runner, then verified by probes — no model — and verified again after the service is recreated.

This run's ledger: 8 checkpoints verified, signed through event 17, checked against ledger.pub beside the ledger; no outside record.

Who wrote what: the harness writes every event; the runner applies only after reading this ledger itself and finding the approval under a signed checkpoint. An approval is only as good as the harness process that wrote it — anyone who controls that process could write one no person gave, and so could anyone able to connect from the front door's own address. The same holds for a verdict: its probes' raw output is the runner's, but the event is the harness's. Approvals signed by the approver's own key would close that; they are not built yet.
