Background
Q: Who is the intended audience for these requirements?
A. The requirements are primarily for:
    •    National Security System (NSS) owners and operators who need to know the
         requirements for their systems
    •    Vendors who need to know what to implement to meet NSS requirements

NSA does not mandate requirements on any other entity outside of NSS. However,
these requirements may serve as guidance for entities outside of NSS, especially where
interoperability requirements or other interests may lead to these recommendations
being used by a broader community.

Q: What is a quantum computer, and how is it different from the computers we
use today?
A: In place of ordinary bits used by today’s computers, quantum computers use “qubits”
that behave in different ways, efficiently performing certain mathematical algorithms
exponentially faster than a classical computer. Small quantum computers are
commercially accessible today.

Q: What is a “cryptanalytically relevant quantum computer” (CRQC)?
A: Also written as “cryptographically relevant quantum computer,” a CRQC describes a
quantum computer capable of attacking real world cryptographic systems. Whether the
“C” indicates “cryptanalytically” or “cryptographically” is a matter of writer’s preference,
as the two terms are essentially equivalent in this context. This term distinguishes a
CRQC from any other “quantum computer” technologies used in other settings without
the performance metrics required to attack real world cryptographic systems.

Q: How would a CRQC pose a threat?
A: If built, a CRQC would be capable of undermining the widely deployed public-key
algorithms currently used for asymmetric key exchanges and digital signatures with
potentially devastating impacts to systems. NSS use public-key cryptography as a
critical component to protect the confidentiality, integrity, and authenticity of national
security information.

Q: Can I continue to use larger sizes of RSA or Elliptic Curve Cryptography (ECC)
to address the threat?
A: No. RSA and ECC are the main algorithms that need to be replaced to achieve
quantum resistance.

Q: What is “quantum-resistant” or “post-quantum” cryptography?
A: “Quantum-resistant” (QR), “quantum-safe,” and “post-quantum” (PQ) cryptography
are all terms to describe cryptographic algorithms that can run on computers today and
are believed to be resistant to cryptanalytic attacks from both classical and quantum
computers. In contrast to QR algorithms, a CRQC will undermine “quantum-vulnerable”
algorithms’ security properties.

Q: What do "full QR" and "partial QR" mean?
A: In current systems, quantum-vulnerable algorithms perform many cryptographic
services. These include confidentiality, authentication, and identity management. As
systems upgrade, some of these services have seen faster QR solution deployment
than others. Many systems offer quantum-resistant encryption before they offer
quantum-resistant identity management, so they will sometimes advertise as "partially
QR." A system is considered "fully QR" when all the cryptographic services it provides
are resistant to cryptanalytic attacks by a CRQC.

CNSA 2.0
Q: Where should CNSA 2.0 algorithms be used?
A: CNSA 2.0 algorithms will be required for all products that employ public-standard
algorithms in NSS, whether for a future design or currently fielded. Any usage of Suite B
or CNSA 1.0 algorithms are required to transition to CNSA 2.0. The Timeframe section
of this FAQ and CNSSP 15 provide transition timeframe information. More details will be
released on an ongoing basis as industry adjusts to the new technology.

Q: How did NSA choose the CNSA 2.0 algorithms?
A: NSA chose algorithms from among those standardized by the National Institute of
Standards and Technology (NIST), the U.S. Government lead for commercial algorithm
approval. NSA believes they offer optimal performance for NSS security requirements.

Q: How strong are CNSA 2.0 algorithms according to NSA?
A: NSA performed its own analysis of CNSA 2.0 algorithms and considers them
appropriate for long-term use in protecting the varied missions of U.S. NSS.

Q: Does NSA intend to produce guidance for CNSA 2.0, similar to the Internet
Engineering Task Force (IETF) Requests for Comments (RFCs) produced for
CNSA 1.0?
A: NSA will provide direction for using CNSA 2.0 algorithms securely in a variety of use
cases and is working with the IETF to produce informational guides for protocol
deployment through the RFC series. RFCs detail protocol options, in addition to
algorithm choices, and NSA expects to provide protocol guidance to ensure smooth and
secure operation with CNSA 2.0 algorithms.

Q: Who is the intended audience for this guidance?
A: These FAQs primarily can assist NSS owners and operators in their transition
planning, and inform industry and vendors of NSS requirements. This guidance is also
useful for those seeking to interoperate with NSS, or for any organization seeking
similar robust security and interoperability requirements.

Q: Does CNSA 2.0 apply to fielded equipment?
A: Even NSS currently in use need to upgrade in a timely fashion unless they receive a
waiver through the approved process. This is consistent with National Security
Presidential Memorandum (NSPM) 121 and National Security Memorandum (NSM) 10,2
as well as CNSSP 113 and CNSSP 15.4

Q: What policies should I follow to meet NSS algorithm requirements?
A: High-grade equipment will follow the guidance in CJCSN 65105 and CNSSAM 01-07-
NSM.6 Commercial equipment will follow CNSSP 15, which mandates transition no later
than 2030; specific deadlines are dependent on equipment type. In accordance with
NSM-10 and CNSSP 11, QR algorithms should be implemented in NSS mission
systems as National Information Assurance Partnership (NIAP) validated products, or in

Q: What should I do if NSA has not published guidance about how to use these
algorithms in my application?
A: While the CNSA 2.0 RFCs published through the IETF, NIAP PPs, and other
guidance documents released by NSA cover a wide range of common technologies, it is
not possible for NSA to provide specific guidance for every application of cryptography.
In particular, cloud-based technologies and specific software toolkits may have their
own names for various cryptographic selections or their own cryptographic protocols.
Developers are expected to determine that the cryptography underlying their choices
complies with CNSA 2.0, but NSA reserves the authority to determine the
appropriateness of these choices if they are not built off certified products. NSA will
respond to NSS users’ specific questions as they arise.

Q: What is the difference between ML-KEM and CRYSTALS-Kyber, or between
ML-DSA and CRYSTALS-Dilithium?
A: ML-KEM and ML-DSA are fully specified standards described by NIST in FIPS 203
and 204, respectively. CRYSTALS-Kyber is the name of the proposed algorithm
submitted to NIST that eventually became ML-KEM, and similarly CRYSTALS-Dilithium
became ML-DSA. In particular, the CRYSTALS algorithms had a variety of versions that
included small tweaks made by the designers in cooperation with NIST prior to the
publication of the FIPS documents. Once NIST announced the drafting of CRYSTALS-
Kyber and CRYSTALS-Dilithium into standards, NSA announced that the standards

would be part of CNSA 2.0. NSA clarified the CNSA 2.0 language when the FIPS
documents were published. Only ML-KEM and ML-DSA are in CNSA 2.0; any algorithm
that is called CRYSTALS-Kyber or CRYSTALS-Dilithium but does not adhere to FIPS
203 or 204 is not CNSA 2.0 compliant.

Q: When should I use hash-based signatures, and which should I use?
A: NSA allows certain NIST SP 800-2087 standardized stateful hash-based signatures
to secure firmware and software and their update processes. Due to the long-expected
lifetime needed for many firmware, they urgently require implementing quantum-
resistant algorithms.

In addition, firmware often must perform in constrained environments. Because many
chips natively support hashing, this makes small hash-based signatures, such as LMS
and XMSS, natural choices. NSA prefers the parameter set in Section 4.2, LMS with
SHA-256/192, but any choice of XMSS or LMS satisfies CNSA 2.0 requirements.

Q: Can I use HSS or XMSSMT from NIST SP 800-208?
A: From NIST SP 800-208, NSA has only approved LMS and XMSS for use in NSS.
The multi-tree algorithms HSS and XMSSMT are not allowed.

Q: Can I use SLH-DSA (aka SPHINCS+) to sign software?
A: While SLH-DSA is hash-based, it is not part of CNSA and is not approved for any
use in NSS.

Q: I'm going to adopt LMS or XMSS for software/firmware validation for NSS.
Which components need to be validated, and how? If my hardware security
module (HSM) is not FIPS-validated, can I get a waiver?
A: Signature verification is expected to be performed by code that has been validated by
NIST’s Cryptographic Algorithm Validation Program (CAVP). It is expected that signed
code may be received from a variety of sources and signers. If your product is only
verifying signatures, CAVP testing is all that is required.

Code sources and signature generation processes (signers) that are NSS are required
to produce signatures according to NIST SP 800-208, which requires hardware

While code on devices used in an NSS must meet CNSA requirements, the signature

involve transferring keys between modules, must prevent state re-use.

Q: As a commercial vendor, how do I know if my NIST SP 800-208 implementation
meets CNSA 2.0?
A: NIAP validates products against its published Protection Profiles, which started
including quantum-resistant signatures consistent with NSA’s published transition
timelines. Where signing occurs outside the Target of Evaluation (TOE) boundary, such
as for commercial products being certified where only firmware signature verification is
performed within the TOE while the firmware signing is performed outside the TOE by
an HSM or other external signing service, Protection Profile requirements only apply to
signature verification. As signature generation is the component of LMS/XMSS that
requires state management, if only signature verification is being performed only CAVP
validation (not CMVP) will be expected to meet CNSA 2.0 for such products.

Q: Why are signatures for software- and firmware-signing listed separately from
signing other information?
A: The reasons for choosing separate algorithms for software- and firmware-signing are
as follows:

    •    NIST standardized the algorithms in NIST SP 800-208 earlier and had CAVP
         validation available for many years, at a time when other quantum-resistant
         signatures were not as easily available for integration. Therefore, they are more
         broadly available in low-level hardware.

    •    This signature use case for firmware and software (especially firmware) is slightly
         more urgent at this time in the acquisition cycle as it must be QR at the time of
         acquisition (see the following question and answer for additional information).

    •    This selection places hash-based algorithms, with their substantial history of
         cryptanalysis, in a use case where their well-described potential performance
         issues—the requirement for keeping track of state and ensuring certain private
         data is not used more than once in signing software or firmware—have minimal
         impact.

Q: Why are firmware signatures more urgent?
A: In many firmware-signing cases the validation algorithm is not easily updated. Thus,
firmware-signing algorithms are frequently locked in for the life of a system. Even in
systems that are designed for extensibility and cryptographic agility, a quantum-
resistant root of trust may be required in the firmware years before the rest of the
system upgrades to quantum-resistance. NSA prioritizes this in its timelines to avoid
unexpected costs and security issues later in the NSS transition.

Q: Under what circumstances is SHA-3 available for use?
A: At a vendor’s discretion, for internal components of a hardware system that do not
interoperate outside a vendor’s environment, SHA3-384 or SHA3-512 may also be used
as part of a system’s integrity-checking process, such as secure boot. This is
specifically being allowed in CNSA 2.0 to speed the transition process when vendor-
specific internal processes make use of the properties of a cryptographic hash to enable
them to transition to the new post-quantum signatures with minimal disruption to their
workflow. In order not to be prescriptive in these cases, and due to the particular threat
vectors inherent in these processes, NSA is allowing the use of either SHA-384, SHA-
512, SHA3-384, or SHA3-512 for these purposes.

Q: Can I use SHA-3 in protocols that validate my virtual machines (VMs)?
A: Hardware is where one expects a hardware root of trust and where SHA-3 is allowed
for use. At this time, VMs chaining back to a virtual root of trust should not use SHA-3 to
cryptographically validate integrity.

Q: Can I use SHA-3 as a hash?
A: No, neither SHA-3 nor SHAKE are approved for use in CNSA 2.0 as a general-
purpose hash algorithm. While NSA allows any parameter set of LMS, including some
that call SHA-3 as an internal function, NSA has not approved SHA-3 as a general-
purpose hash algorithm. Its use is strictly limited to those cases where it is prescribed
by the standard describing an NSA-approved algorithm, such as LMS within NIST SP
800-208, or for internal hardware system processes as described above.

The SHA-2 selections are sufficient for security, and their ubiquity in the commercial
world ensures interoperability. Using SHA-3 or SHAKE outside those narrowly defined
applications where it is completely self-contained, such as when called within an
algorithm function, significantly increases the interoperability testing burden and breaks
many use cases for CNSA 2.0.

Q: Can I use SHA-3 and SHA-256 for self-contained non-hash purposes,
specifically as a pseudo-random function in deterministic random bit generators
(DRBGs) and key derivation functions (KDFs), or as an alternative to XOR for
combining submasks?
A: No. Unlike other algorithms in CNSA 2.0, no explicit carve outs say a specific DRBG
is allowed, hence these higher-level primitives should be composed of CNSA 2.0
hashes or block ciphers. If there are specific places where SHA-3 or another hash is
appropriate and approved, this will be clearly documented in NSA guidance, as was
done with secure boot.

Q: Are there any plans to add SHA-3/SHAKE to the "General Purpose Algorithms"
section of CNSA?
A: While there are no current plans to allow SHA-3/SHAKE on a wider scale, with SHA-
3’s current standing as a component in many primitives and its increasing adoption in
networking standards, it would be prudent from a cryptographic agility perspective for
NSS customers to have larger versions of SHA-3/SHAKE available in FIPS 140
validated products.

Q: Where can I learn more about lattice-based key encapsulation mechanisms

A: NIST’s post-quantum standardization page includes reports from all the rounds of the

Q: Why did NSA choose ML-DSA over FN-DSA (aka Falcon)?
A: For NSS, NSA agrees with NIST: ML-DSA is preferred, as FN-DSA seems more
susceptible to implementation errors that may affect security. Since NIST has prioritized
standardizing ML-DSA, FN-DSA is not available yet, and deployment of CNSA 2.0
needs to commence as soon as possible, NSA selected ML-DSA. NSA does not
anticipate adding FN-DSA to CNSA 2.0 when it is available.

Q: Can I use ML-DSA for firmware or software signing?
A: Yes, however, firmware roots of trust are a critical component to upgrade and NSA
expects this to be implemented at the present time for some long-lived signatures.
Given validated LMS and XMSS have been commercially available longer, NSA wants
to ensure this transition can begin now (using LMS and XMSS) whenever possible,
rather than wait for ML-DSA due to the long timeframes involved in moving from small
components and/or early designs to completed products.

Validated ML-DSA is approved for all signing use cases, and it may be the most
appropriate choice for some software/firmware signing use cases. For example, when a
user’s software signing strategy requires more signatures than can be reasonably used
with a single LMS or XMSS key, or in software development environments with a
distributed signing system, it would be reasonable to use ML-DSA.

breaking interoperability with ML-DSA to prevent a vulnerability in the case of key re-
use. HashML-DSA offers no additional functionality over existing CNSA hash functions
combined in a standard way with ML-DSA-87, which is expected to be widely

Timeframe
Q: What is NSA’s recommended timeframe for CNSA 2.0 adoption?
A: NSA intends that all NSS will be quantum-resistant well ahead of the 2035 goal
outlined in NSM-10. The update to CNSSP 15 set several dates to aid in this transition

By December 31, 2030, all NSS equipment and services that cannot support CNSA 2.0
must be phased out unless otherwise granted an exception. Because of how negotiation
is performed in modern protocols, this is also the date at which NSA expects encryption
to be broadly QR by default.

By December 31, 2031, CNSA 2.0 algorithms are mandated for use, unless otherwise
noted, to achieve full QR for NSS.

be required to support CNSA 2.0 algorithms once appropriate standards for the given
technology are in place. All appropriate system components should be configured to
prefer CNSA 2.0 algorithms. As products mature, those components should be
configured to accept only CNSA 2.0 algorithms.
NSA will provide guidance and updated Protection Profiles as Standard Development
Organizations (SDOs) develop the appropriate standards because product lines may
develop at different speeds, and not every profile supporting CNSA 2.0 will support it for
every cryptographic service offered. CNSA 1.0 algorithms will continue to be used until
current solutions can operate in a CNSA 2.0 mode.

Q: What is National Security Memorandum (NSM) 10?

Q: Can I mitigate the quantum threat to NSS by using a pre-shared key?

quantum threat, and some allow the combination of pre-shared and asymmetric keys in
the same negotiation. However, this issue can be complex and using pre-shared keys
or a combination of keys may be prohibited by policy for some customers. Customers
who wish to explore this option for their long-lived or low-powered systems should
contact NSA or follow guidance the CSfC program provides.

Q: Will quantum computers affect non-public-key (i.e., symmetric) algorithms?
A: Quantum computing techniques are generally considered much less effective against
symmetric algorithms than against current widely used public-key algorithms. While
public-key cryptography requires fundamental design changes, symmetric algorithms
are considered secure, provided the key size is sufficiently large. CNSA 2.0 symmetric
algorithms, which essentially are the same as their CNSA 1.0 counterparts, are
quantum-resistant.

Q: Why does NSA care about quantum computing today? Isn’t quantum
computing a long way off?
A: NSM-10 documents specific requirements for NSA to provide quantum resistance for
NSS by 2035, requiring significant steps over the next several years. Further,
government procurement timelines drive NSA to strongly encourage and support
commercial QR technology availability by 2027.

NSA does not know when there will be a CRQC. Expert assessments disagree
significantly about timing. Because NSS often have very long lifecycles, NSA must
produce requirements today for systems that will be used many decades in the future.
Consequently, the data these systems protect will still require cryptographic protection
for decades after these systems are at end of life. Research on quantum computing is
progressing, so NSA must act now to protect NSS by providing these CNSA 2.0
transition requirements and guidance today.

Q: I just read a news report or scientific paper asserting quantum computers are
coming soon. Is NSA’s timeline correct?
A: As with any emerging technology, breakthroughs, claims, and new barriers to
progress are to be expected and will continue to appear. This is an evolving area with
many opinions. NSA monitors work in this area and believes the currently established
timelines are the most responsible choice given where customers are today with the
long-term mitigations. NSA will notify the NSS community if changes to timelines are
required, but NSA does not anticipate any changes at this time. Other communities may
have different circumstances and may follow different timelines.

Q: Given the threat, is the NSS community moving aggressively enough to
mitigate the impact of an early CRQC?
A: NSA has been at the forefront of mitigating the quantum threat to cryptography for
years. NSA believes that the currently established timelines are appropriate to address
the expected threat. Since 2016, small elliptic curves have been deprecated in NSS.
With the signing of CNSSP 15 in 2025, exemptions to this were revoked. Currently,
compliant devices should be using either Curve P-384, RSA with at least a 3072-bit
modulus, or an NSA-approved alternative. A sufficiently capable CRQC would be
necessary to undermine these algorithms and key sizes. Further, the transition to QR
algorithms is already underway and proceeding with urgency. Starting in 2027, no new
product for NSS should have a burned-in root of trust relying on a non-QR primitive. By
the end of 2030, all available devices should fully support QR algorithms.

Q: What is quantum key distribution (QKD)?
A: The field of quantum cryptography involves specialized hardware using the physics
of quantum mechanics to protect the confidentiality of sensitive information. The most
common example today uses quantum physics to distribute keys for use in a traditional
symmetric algorithm, known as “quantum key distribution” or QKD. This technology
exists today and is distinct from the quantum computing technology that might one day
attack cryptographic algorithms. The sole function of QKD is to distribute keys between
users. Hence, it is only one part of a cryptographic system.

Q: Can I use a QKD system to protect my national security system from a
quantum computer?
A: No. The technology involved is of significant scientific interest, but it only addresses
some security threats and requires significant engineering modifications to NSS
communications systems. NSA does not generally consider QKD a practical security
solution for protecting NSS. NSS owners should not use or research QKD at this time
without consulting NSA directly. For specific questions, NSS owners can contact NSA.

Q: What is a quantum random number generator?
A: A quantum random number generator (RNG), also called a Quantum RNG or a
QRNG, is a hardware RNG that uses specific quantum effects to generate
nondeterministic randomness. CNSS policy does not favor quantum RNGs versus other

depends on many factors. In addition, any properly certified/approved RNG should be

Hybrids and composites
Q: What is a hybrid or composite cryptographic solution?
A: NSA views a hybrid solution for a protocol as one that uses multiple algorithms to
perform the same function, such as key agreement or authentication. The solution uses
algorithms in a way that requires an adversarial actor to break each one to compromise
system security. Hybrid solutions can consist of many traditional or QR algorithms.
“Component algorithms” are individual algorithms used in a hybrid solution. In some




U/OO/6072712-26 | PP-26-3194 | October 2026 Ver. 2.2                                    27
  NSA | CNSA Suite 2.0 and Quantum Computing FAQ



circumstances, such as in forums discussing public key certificates, the word
"composite" is used instead. This document uses the two terms interchangeably.

Q: What is NSA’s position on the use of hybrid solutions?
A: NSA has confidence in CNSA 2.0 algorithms and will not require NSS developers to
use hybrid certified products for security purposes. However, product availability and
interoperability requirements may lead to adopting hybrid solutions in specific cases.

NSA recognizes that some protocols may require using hybrid-like constructions to
accommodate the larger sizes of ML-KEM-1024 or ML-DSA-87 and will work with
industry and SDOs to identify the best options for implementation. In these cases,
where NSA worked with SDO and provided public guidance on hybrid use, only the
hybrid solution identified by NSA would be considered “CNSA 2.0 compliant.”

Q: If I use a hybrid solution in my NSS, am I quantum resistant?
A: NSS is only considered compliant and quantum resistant when using CNSA 2.0
algorithms in an NSA-approved manner, such as when validated against an appropriate
NIAP Protection Profile. While hybrid standards that use NIST standards exist in many
SDOs, very few use CNSA 2.0 algorithms. Moreover, design choices of whether to
negotiate a hybrid, what format to have in a certificate, etc. are often unclear. As a
result, something marketed as hybrid or composite does not necessarily make it
quantum resistant.

Q: What complications do hybrid solutions introduce?
A: Hybrid solutions add complexity to protocols and cryptographic libraries, as designers
need to incorporate additional negotiation and error handling and implementers need to
modify APIs and testing.

Rather than ease the transition to quantum resistance, hybrid deployments can
introduce additional interoperability concerns because all the algorithms and the method
of hybridization must be features common to all parties in communication. Similarly,
hybrid deployments add a second transition later as users eventually move away from
classical algorithms entirely in the future and use only quantum-resistant algorithms.

At the same time, hybrid solutions make the implementations more complex, so one
must balance the risk of flaws in an increasingly complex implementation with the risk of



U/OO/6072712-26 | PP-26-3194 | October 2026 Ver. 2.2                                  28
  NSA | CNSA Suite 2.0 and Quantum Computing FAQ



a cryptanalytic breakthrough. Because more security products fail due to
implementation or configuration errors than failures in their underlying cryptographic
algorithms, spending limited resources to add cryptographic complexity can weaken
security rather than improve it. Indeed, for some formal security properties, hybrids may
be no stronger, or possibly even weaker, than their individual components. This is a
matter of significant debate in industry and academia.

In many scenarios, adding a hybrid option will require the standards community to
determine non-obvious choices, such as how to negotiate hybrid algorithms or how to
combine keys securely, which may slow down the process of standardization past
NSA’s deployment goals.

When NSA recognizes a need to support a hybrid solution, extensive work is performed
to ensure that it can be expected to be implemented properly, including engineering the
solution to a high degree of robustness.

Q: Is there an example where NSA will recommend hybrid solutions?
A: Due to difficulties introduced when unencrypted IKEv2 messages exceed a certain
byte size, it is not possible to directly replace the CNSA 1.0 public key algorithms with
ML-KEM-1024 and its larger public key. Because there is no straightforward way to
construct a solution that uses a standalone ML-KEM-1024 key establishment without
dramatic changes to this part of IKEv2, NSA approved using the solution standardized
within the IETF. This solution effectively manages this problem by performing a smaller
key establishment, followed by a larger, but encrypted, key establishment. As a result,
for the foreseeable future, NSA’s CNSA 2.0 Profile of this solution will continue using
CNSA 1.0 key establishment algorithms but fortified by key establishment using ML-
KEM-1024 from CNSA 2.0.

should not use novel mechanisms to support multiple algorithms within a single
certificate.

Q: Should one use a hybrid or other non-standardized QR solution while waiting
for a final post-quantum protocol standard for any specific technologies?
A: Do not use any non-standardized QR solution on NSS mission systems except for
those exceptions NSA specifically recommends to meet standardization or
interoperability requirements. Because NSA is confident that CNSA 2.0 algorithms will
sufficiently protect NSS, it does not require a hybrid solution for security purposes.
Importantly, it is unlikely a non-standardized solution used in testing will be able to be
integrated into eventual deployable solutions.

Using non-standard solutions entails a significant risk of establishing incompatible
solutions. Using a hybrid solution that involves a symmetric key in accordance with
established standards (e.g., RFC 8773, RFC 8784) may be appropriate, but key
management complexity generally restricts this to specialized applications.
