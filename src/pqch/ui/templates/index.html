A cryptographic inventory built from evidence, kept on a signed ledger, and used to plan the migration: what depends on what, what comes first, and in what order. The sections below go from a stale spreadsheet to that plan; carrying out a step of it is a separate stage, resolution. Where the models are: reading a call site, naming an unfamiliar spelling, resolving a conflict between findings — each role given to a model only where measurement showed it earned it. Scanners, rules and probes do the rest, and no model decides whether a change worked.

Why the clock matters

The inventory is required now, in the discovery phase, and what a federal CBOM must contain has not been published: CISA and NIST are to set its minimum elements by 2027-03-19. So this workbench keeps every finding with its evidence on a ledger and treats the CBOM as a projection of that record. When the minimum elements arrive, meeting them is a new projection of the same record — a rebuild, not a re-scan. Key establishment is the first queue because traffic recorded today can be decrypted once the key exchange is broken. What federal policy requires, and by when.

What a CBOM is, and what this one holds

A Cryptography Bill of Materials is a CycloneDX document — an Ecma standard, ECMA-424 — whose components are cryptographic assets: algorithms, certificates, protocols and key material, each with its properties and its dependencies. The format is settled; what an agency must put in one is not, until CISA and NIST publish the minimum elements in 2027. This CBOM adds what the standard leaves open under a pqch: namespace: which layer of evidence each entry came from, which tool, the ledger event holding the raw output and its hash, and whether a rule judged it in use. It is rebuilt from the ledger and never edited. What is a CBOM, and what is in one?
